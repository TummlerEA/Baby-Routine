Help

🚀 Using this app

The three buttons

Tap Feed, Nappy or Sleep at the moment it happens. One tap is the whole job — the entry is saved straight away, and you can put the phone down.

The button tells you it worked: for a few seconds it reads Logged with the time it went in. If you are not sure whether a tap registered, look at the button rather than tapping again.

Sleep is a toggle. Tap it when your baby goes down, and it changes to Wake up; tap it again when they wake, and the app works out how long they slept.

Below them, Woke → nappy change → feeding is one tap for that whole sequence rather than three: it logs a wake-up ten minutes ago, a nappy change (wee & poo) five minutes ago, and a feed right now, recorded as having taken fifteen minutes — five minutes apart, ending at this moment. Undo on the confirmation removes all three at once; if any of that was not quite right this time, correct each entry afterwards the same way as any other.

The card that appears after a tap

  • When did this happen? Logging is often a catch-up — the baby woke, a nappy, a feed, back to sleep, and the phone only comes out at the end. This time wheel opens already sitting on the moment just logged; wind it back instead of a trip to the history, and the button itself updates to match. No date on it — it is always today, and correcting something onto a different day is what the manual form below is for. Waking up opens this card too, just for this — there is nothing to plan from a wake-up, but it is usually the first thing that needs backdating.
  • It shows when the next one is due, with a row of alternatives. Tapping one changes only that gap — useful for a long night stretch — and leaves your everyday plan alone.
  • After a nappy it also asks what was in it: wee, poo, both or dry. Answering is optional. Tap the same answer twice to clear it if you mis-tap.
  • If you log the same thing twice within three minutes it says so — "that is the 2nd feed in three minutes" — and offers Undo, which removes that entry and leaves everything else alone. It never refuses the entry: two feeds close together is unusual, not impossible.
  • It disappears on its own after twenty seconds, or with the ✕.

What's next

Predictions come from the intervals you set in Settings → Planned intervals, three hours by default. That means they match your routine from the very first entry, rather than waiting for a pattern to emerge.

Each one also shows what is actually happening. If it says actually averaging 4h 30m, your real routine has drifted from the plan — change the plan, or don't; the app is not going to nag.

Handover (🤝)

For the moment somebody takes over — the other parent coming off a night, a grandparent, a nanny arriving at the door. It answers, in sentences, what they are about to ask: when the last feed was and how long it took, how many nappies and of what kind, how much sleep there has been, what is due next, and anything measured along the way.

Above the words is the same stretch drawn as a strip: feeds on the top rail, nappies in the middle, sleep along the bottom, with time running left to right and ending at this minute. The darker bands are night. A timed feed is drawn as long as it took.

Choose 6, 12 or 24 hours. It is always counted back from right now, never from midnight: at nine in the morning "today" is three hours old, which tells whoever has just arrived nothing about the night that has just gone.

English or Russian. A second row of buttons switches the whole screen — the sentences, the dates, the counts and the temperature advice — between the two. The buttons are marked en and ru rather than with the languages' own names, so the chip that fixes an unreadable screen reads the same whichever language you have. The choice belongs to the handset and is shared with the shopping list below: a carer can read Russian on both her screens while yours stay in English, and it never travels over sync, a backup or a share link. Only these two screens change; the rest of the app is English for now. Names, appointments and anything else somebody typed stay exactly as they typed them — the app translates nobody's words, which would take a service to ask, and it asks nobody anything.

None of it leaves the phone. It is a page to read together, not something to send.

Shopping list (🛒)

Either box on its own is enough to add something. Type a name and tapping Amazon searches Amazon UK for it; paste a link instead — a full one, or a short amzn.eu one — and tapping Open hands it straight to the Amazon app if one is installed. Give only a link and the item shows as the link itself, in italics, until you rename it: there is no way to ask Amazon for a product's title without making a request to their server, which this app does not do.

Before something is bought, a small pill shows where it stands: New, Ordered or Arrived. Tapping it moves it on a step, wrapping back to New so a mis-tap is one more tap from undone. Buying does not depend on it — tick something off from any status, New included.

Once something is Ordered, a date field appears beside the pill for when it is expected — optional, and only there while it is on order. Set it and everyone who opens the list sees it too, the same way as everything else here. Moving off Ordered, either way, clears it, so a later re-order never inherits a date left over from the first one.

Tick something off when it is bought. It stays crossed out under Bought for a day, so a mis-tap is easy to undo, then it clears itself.

The list lives behind the ⋯ at the end of the top row, along with this help screen — the two screens opened least often, tucked away so the row of buttons holds one line on a small phone. The number on ⋯ is how many items are still outstanding, and the handover screen names them — the two places you are already looking. There is still no way for this app to actually notify anybody: an item added at eight is seen whenever the other person next opens the app.

Reads in English or Russian, the same choice and the same button as the handover screen above. Whatever anybody types stays exactly as typed.

Carer rota (🤗)

One week on screen at a time, with arrows either side of the date range to move to another. Tap a day to step it forward: nothing set → Off → working, and a third tap clears it back to nothing set. Marking a day off is always one tap away, from wherever it currently stands.

Once a day is working, two dropdowns appear — hours and a start time — pre-set to 6 hours and 9:00 rather than left blank, since changing a sensible default is quicker than typing one in from nothing. The start time moves in half-hour steps, not one minute at a time.

Every shift is its own date: how long a day runs and when it starts can differ from one week to the next, and there is no repeating pattern behind any of it to fight with when the real week does not match last week's. The total for the week on screen sits next to the date range.

A start time buys a quiet line on the main screen while that shift is on — Carer here until the time it ends. A shift with no start time has nothing to count down to, so it stays quiet. Nothing here clocks anyone in or out, and there is no separate record of who actually turned up when — it is settled between the carer and the other parent, not by this app, and this just keeps the shape of it in one place, synced like everything else.

Statistics (📊)

Three small charts — feeds, nappies and sleep, one bar per day — over the last 7, 14 or 30 days. Drawn from the same log as everything else, so there is nothing extra to keep in step. A dashed line marks the average across the whole chart, so a bar reads against it rather than in isolation, and on the 7 and 14-day views each bar also carries its own number. Past that a bar is small enough that a number on it would just crowd — the dashed line still shows the shape. A line under each chart gives the exact daily average and the total for the range.

Above them, Nights — the longest unbroken stretch and how many times you were up, one bar per night. This is the only part of the app that measures a night rather than a day, and the difference matters: a night starts in the evening and ends the next morning, so midnight falls in the middle of it and a calendar day cuts it in half. Where exactly to cut is set under ⚙️ Settings, beside the planned intervals — cluster feeding until nine is one family's evening and another's night, so the app does not guess. A night still in progress is left out rather than drawn as a short bar.

Below them, measurements over time — weight, length, head circumference, temperature, and anything you have named yourself — one chart each, as soon as there are two readings of it to join up. These keep their own timeline and the buttons at the top do not apply to them: readings are weeks apart rather than daily, so each one sits where it actually falls on the calendar and a month with no weighing shows as a month-wide gap. The value scale runs from the lowest reading to the highest rather than from zero, because a baby going from 3.2 to 5.2 kg is the whole story and against a zero line it would be a flat line near the top; both ends are labelled, and the unit is in the heading. Tap and hold a dot to see its date and reading.

Nothing is sent anywhere to draw these — they are worked out on the phone from what is already logged.

Weight

The card shows the latest weight in kilos and in pounds and ounces, what it changed by since the weighing before, and where that sits against birth weight — the figure watched closely in the first fortnight, when a newborn normally dips before climbing back.

It also gives grams a day between the last two weighings, which is what a midwife or health visitor asks for and is not something you can work out in your head from "+300 g" and two dates. It needs about a day between the weighings to mean anything, so a baby weighed twice in one afternoon is not reported as gaining a kilo a day. Weight only — nobody quotes a daily rate for a head circumference.

Sleep entries with no pair

A sleep needs both halves to have a length — "fell asleep" and "woke up". If one is missing, that entry cannot be measured and is left out of every sleep total, including the nights on 📊. It happens: the wake-up gets logged twice, or not at all, or Sleep is tapped by accident.

When there are any, a panel above the history lists them, newest first, with what is missing from each. Tap one to open it — correct its time if it was right but mistimed, or delete it if it was a mis-tap. Add the entry that was missing and the panel goes away on its own. A sleep that is simply still going is not listed: it has no wake-up yet because your baby has not woken up yet.

How long since the last one

Every feed and every nappy in the history says how long it had been since the last one of the same kind — "2h 14m since the last feed" or "45 mins since the last nappy". Feeds count from feeds and nappies from nappies, so a nappy change in between does not reset a feed's gap.

It is measured start to start, which is what "every three hours" means to anybody saying it: a feed at nine that ran twenty minutes and the next at ten are an hour apart, not forty minutes. The gap is counted across the whole log rather than within a day, so the long overnight stretch — the one that actually matters — is measured properly instead of being cut in half at midnight. Two taps inside the same minute are treated as the slip they usually are and get no figure.

Sleep is left out: it already carries how long it lasted, and the gap between one sleep and the next is time awake, which is a different thing that would read as the same one — it gets its own line instead, below.

Time spent awake

Every "Fell asleep" entry says how long your baby had been awake beforehand — "awake 1h 40m", measured from the "Woke up" entry right before it to this one. It is the mirror of a sleep's own length, and it only appears once both entries are there to measure between.

📊 Statistics adds a fourth chart for it, alongside feeds, nappies and sleep — "Awake per day". A stretch that runs past midnight is split correctly between the two days rather than counted whole against one of them, the same way the sleep chart already handles it. Only awake time properly bounded by a wake-up and the fell-asleep after it is counted, so the very first stretch of a baby's life and any run that has not ended yet — awake right now, no nap logged since — count too, up to the moment you look, same as an ongoing sleep already does.

A daily routine, behind ⏰

The planned intervals under ⚙️ Settings answer "how long since the last one". They cannot answer "what should be happening now", and that is the question a day that has slid needs — an overtired baby who would not go down, a nap that ran to four hours, a night that started two hours late. ⏰ Daily routine is the shape of the day by the clock instead: when each sleep starts, when it is meant to end, and where the feeds fall between them.

It arrives switched off and filled in, with three sleeps overnight and four naps through the day. Turn it on and the main screen gains a line saying what comes next and how long your baby has actually been awake against it. Nothing else changes: 📈 What's next keeps working from the intervals exactly as before.

A sleep carries both of its ends, and the gap between one ending and the next beginning is the stretch awake the app holds the day to — taken from your own routine rather than asked for as another setting, which is why the evening stretch is allowed to run longer than the ones between daytime naps.

Which of the two it quotes is the whole of how it works. Within half an hour either way, the table wins: it says "Sleep at 12:00 by the routine", because hearing the same handful of hours day after day is how a household learns them, and a quarter of an hour is not a difference a baby notices. Further off than that, the stretch awake wins and the honest time is named instead — a nap the routine wants at noon cannot be taken by a baby who woke at ten to, and quoting noon anyway would only teach that the app is wrong. When it does that it says what the routine had wanted, and names the one wake-up that puts the rest of the day back on it: "up by 14:00 and the day is back on it".

Under 📈 What's next the day itself is laid out four steps at a time — the one just gone, the one in hand, and the two after it — with what actually happened beside each: "slept 1h 50m", "40m late", "not logged". The heading says whether the day is on plan or how far behind it is running. The whole table is one tap away on the ⏰ screen.

Past the stretch it turns amber, and further past it red. That is the whole point of it — a baby who has been up too long does not go down easily, and the app has no way to tap you on the shoulder. It cannot send a notification: it has no server and no background of its own, so it can only say this while you are looking at it.

None of it is enforced. A baby who wakes early or is hungry early is fed early, and nothing here stops you or marks you down for it. "Today so far" counts the naps and night sleeps you have actually logged against the number the routine asks for — which side of the night a sleep falls on comes from where you put the night under ⚙️ Settings, so the two never disagree. The routine is a setting, not a log: it rides sync and the JSON backup with the rest of them, and changes nothing about what has already been recorded.

Fixing mistakes

  • Tap any entry in the history to change its type, time or details.
  • Deleting gives you seven seconds to undo.
  • Add or edit an entry lets you record something you forgot, at the time it really happened.

History and measurements

History groups entries by day. Today and yesterday are open; older days are one tap away. Each day header totals that day's feeds, nappies — including wet and dirty counts — and sleep.

Measurements holds weight, length, head circumference and temperature — the three growth measurements are the ones plotted on centile charts in your baby's health record (the red book, in the UK). They are not on the big buttons because they are not things you log half asleep. Weight shows in kilograms and in pounds and ounces, with the change since last time and against birth weight.

Something else takes any other reading you are handed — a bilirubin result, a bottle volume, anything with a number. Name it and give it a unit once, and that name is offered back next time, so each thing builds its own little history. The app records and shows these and interprets nothing: unlike temperature, there is no general safe range for an arbitrary test, and reference ranges differ by laboratory and by age.

Note is for the same drawer, for anything that isn't a number — a rash, say. Say what you noticed and, optionally, paste a link to a photo of it. No photo ever lives in this app; the link points at wherever you actually keep it — a shared Google Drive or iCloud album works well, and Settings can hold that album's link so it is offered on every new note without retyping it. Set to share the album by link at the Drive or iCloud end, once, and it works the same for whoever else opens the log. A note syncs and backs up in full, the same as everything else — only a share link leaves it out, since there is no room there for its text.

⚠ Keep a backup

Everything is stored in this browser and nowhere else. There is no account and no server — which is why nothing about your baby ever leaves your phone, and equally why clearing your browser data or changing phone will destroy it all.

Every so often: Settings → Export & backup → Download JSON. Restoring from that file brings back your entries, your planned intervals and the date of birth.

Restoring merges rather than replaces, so you can send the file to another phone and import it there. Each entry remembers when it last changed: the newer version of an entry wins, deletions carry across, and importing an out-of-date file cannot undo newer work.

Sharing with your partner

Settings → Share with your partner makes a link holding your recent entries. Send it however you like; when they open it, their app asks before merging anything.

It works both ways — whatever each of you logged ends up in both logs, edits and deletions included, and the newer version of an entry always wins. Send a fresh link whenever you swap over.

Setting them up the first time? Use Invite your partner in the same place. It sends the address of the app along with a short note explaining how the two of you will swap entries. Your baby's name and its style, the date of birth, how your baby is fed and the planned intervals travel across with the first batch of entries, so they do not have to type any of it in.

An iPhone quirk worth knowing. A web app added to the Home Screen keeps its data entirely separately from Safari, and iOS always opens a tapped link in Safari — never in the Home Screen app. So a link tapped from a chat lands in Safari, and its entries stay there.

Pick one and stay with it:

  • Using Safari? Just tap the links. Everything lands in the same place.
  • Added it to your Home Screen? Don't tap the link — hold it, copy it, open the app and paste it into Settings → Share. If you have already opened one in Safari, the card there has a button that copies the link ready for pasting.

The entries travel inside the link itself and are never uploaded, which also means anyone holding the link can read them. Keep the period short: a couple of days stays comfortably shareable, while whole months make a link long enough that messaging apps mangle it. For the full history, send the JSON backup instead.

Keeping two phones in step automatically

Swapping links works, but it is manual. Settings → Sync across phones keeps both phones in step through a private repository on GitHub — no server of ours in the middle, and nobody else can read it.

Set it up once on each phone:

  • Make a private repository on GitHub. An empty one is fine; the app creates the file it needs.
  • Make a fine-grained access token at Settings → Developer settings → Personal access tokens, give it access to only that repository, and set Contents: Read and write. A classic token would reach every repository you own — do not use one.
  • Paste the repository as owner/name and the token into Settings, and press Connect.

After that it syncs when you open the app, when you come back to it, about once a minute while it is on screen, and shortly after anything you log. If both of you write at the same moment, nothing is lost: the newer version of each entry wins and the rest is merged.

The settings travel too — the baby's name and its style, the date of birth, how your baby is fed, and the planned intervals. A phone connecting for the first time takes them from the log it is joining, even if somebody had already typed a name into it, so a new phone never renames the baby on everybody else's. After that the most recent change wins, whichever phone made it.

The token stays on the phone you typed it into. It is never put in a backup, a report or a share link. If a phone is lost, delete that token on GitHub and the phone loses access.

About deleted entries. A deleted entry leaves a marker behind rather than vanishing, because a row that is simply missing looks the same as one the other phone has not seen yet — and would come back on the next sync. The marker keeps only the identity and the time, never the reading or the nappy detail, and it is thrown away by every phone once it is a month old. So a deletion always travels, and then tidies itself up.

One caveat worth knowing: a repository keeps its history, so earlier versions of the file remain in past commits even after the current one is cleaned. If you ever need something truly gone, delete the repository and connect a fresh one.

Putting it on your home screen

On iPhone, open the app in Safari, tap the share button, then Add to Home Screen. It then opens like an ordinary app, full screen, and keeps working without a signal.

💡 For new parents

Reading your baby's sleep

Newborns spend about half their sleep in active sleep, and it can look alarming when you don't know what you're seeing.

  • Active sleep (REM). Eyelids flutter, the face makes grimaces and half-smiles, arms and legs twitch, breathing is irregular, and there are little grunts and squeaks. Your baby is asleep. Picking them up now often wakes them properly — most babies settle again on their own.
  • Quiet sleep. The body is still, the face relaxed, breathing steady and even. This is the deep phase, and they are much harder to wake.

Sleep cycles are short — roughly 40 to 50 minutes — so babies surface frequently. That is normal, not a sign anything is wrong.

Checking they're breathing — without waking them

  • Watch the chest and tummy rise and fall for half a minute. This is usually all you need, and it wakes nobody.
  • If you want more reassurance, warm your hand first, then rest it very lightly on their chest or back. A cold hand or firm pressure will wake them.
  • Do it during quiet sleep if you can. In active sleep the twitching and uneven breathing can worry you for no reason.

What's normal for a newborn: around 30 to 60 breaths a minute, and an uneven rhythm — a few quick breaths, then a pause of up to about 5 seconds, then breathing resumes. This is called periodic breathing and it settles as they grow.

⚠ When to call an ambulance straight away

  • A pause in breathing longer than 20 seconds, or any pause where they go pale, blue, grey or floppy
  • Blue or grey lips, tongue or face
  • Struggling to breathe: nostrils flaring, grunting with every breath, or the skin sucking in under and between the ribs
  • Limp, unresponsive, or very difficult to wake
  • A temperature of 38°C or higher in a baby under 3 months

999 in the UK. 112 reaches an ambulance across Europe, including Switzerland. Anywhere else, look up your own number now and keep it where you will find it in the dark — not while this is happening.

For anything that worries you but isn't an emergency, call NHS 111 if you are in the UK, or speak to your midwife, health visitor, doctor or paediatrician. You know your baby — if something feels wrong, get it checked.

Safer sleep basics

  • On their back for every sleep, day and night
  • Feet at the foot of the cot, with blankets tucked no higher than the shoulders
  • A firm, flat mattress. No pillows, duvets, cot bumpers or soft toys
  • Room temperature between 16°C and 20°C, with light layers or a sleeping bag. No hat indoors
  • Sleep in the same room as your baby for the first 6 months
  • Never sleep with your baby on a sofa or an armchair
  • Keep the room and the car smoke-free

This follows the guidance from The Lullaby Trust and the NHS, which are worth reading in full.

Feeds and nappies, roughly

  • Newborns commonly feed 8 to 12 times in 24 hours, and rarely on a tidy schedule. Several feeds bunched together in the evening is normal.
  • From about day 5, six or more properly wet nappies a day is a good sign they're getting enough.
  • Steady weight gain and wet nappies tell you far more than any clock does.

The predictions in this app are simply an average of what your baby has already done — they are a pattern, not a schedule. Feed your baby when they ask, not when the countdown says so. This page is general information, not medical advice.

Puts a button on the main screen that hands a summary of the log to Claude, ChatGPT or Perplexity — whichever you already use. The app has no AI of its own and never will; this only saves you typing the numbers out.

Off, the summary says "the baby" — the answer is just as good and one less thing has left the phone. Nothing else identifying is ever included, and your sync token never is.

You see the exact text before anything is sent, and you can stop at copying it. This setting stays on this phone.

🤝 Share with your partner

Sending them here for the first time? Start with an invitation — it explains what the app is and how the two of you will swap entries.

Once they have it, send a link holding your recent entries. Their app offers to merge it into theirs — entries you both added end up in both logs.

Been sent a link? If you use this app from your Home Screen, do not tap the link — that opens Safari, which keeps its own separate copy of your data. Hold the link, copy it, then paste it here.

The entries travel inside the link itself and are never uploaded anywhere — but that also means anyone holding the link can read them, so send it only to someone you trust with it. For the whole history, use the JSON backup below instead.

🔄 Sync across phones

Keeps both phones in step automatically through a private repository on GitHub. Nobody else can read it, and there is still no server of ours in the middle.

Not connected

Create the token at github.com → Settings → Developer settings → Personal access tokens → Fine-grained tokens. Give it access to only that one repository, with Contents: Read and write. A classic token would reach every repository you own — do not use one here.

The token stays on this phone and is never included in a backup, a report or a share link. Both phones need connecting once each.

💾 Export & backup

Everything is stored in this browser only. Clearing site data or changing phone will delete it, so save a backup now and then. Restoring adds entries to what you already have and skips anything it has seen before.
