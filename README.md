# Baby Tracker 👶

A mobile web app for tracking a newborn's three basic needs: **feeds**, **nappies** and **sleep**. It predicts when the next one is due from the typical interval between past entries.

Live version: **https://tummlerea.github.io/Baby-Routine/**

## Features

- Three large one-tap buttons: Feed, Nappy, and Sleep (which toggles to Wake up while your baby is asleep).
- **Age, right next to the date.** Once a date of birth is set, the header shows it in days for the first fortnight, then weeks, then months — the same wording already used in Settings, now visible without opening it.
- **One tap for the sequence that is usually three.** Below the three buttons, **Woke → nappy change → feeding** logs all three at once — a wake-up ten minutes ago, a nappy change (wee & poo) five minutes ago, a feed right now recorded as a fifteen-minute feed — five minutes apart, ending at this moment, instead of three separate taps for something that happens together most mornings. **Undo** on the confirmation removes all three as one; if any of that was not quite right this time, each entry can still be corrected afterwards like any other.
- **The button says it worked.** After a tap it reads "Logged 14:32" for a few seconds, on the button itself — the card below already named the entry, but the eye is on the button that was just pressed, and a quarter-second flash is easy to miss in the dark with a baby in the other arm. The space for that line is reserved whether or not there is anything in it, so nothing shifts under the finger about to make the next tap. Second and further entries of the same kind within three minutes are counted out on the card — "that is the 3rd feed in three minutes" — with **Undo** beside it, which removes that entry and nothing else. Nothing is ever blocked: two feeds close together is unlikely, not impossible, and the person holding the baby decides. The form confirms the same way, naming the time it saved and refusing a second press for a moment, which is where two identical entries used to come from.
- **What was in the nappy** — wee, poo, both or dry. The entry is saved the instant you tap Nappy, and the question appears in the prompt that follows, so answering it is optional and never stands between you and a one-handed tap in the dark. Day summaries then show the wet and dirty counts a health visitor asks about.
- **Predictions built from your plan, not from averages.** You set how often you intend each thing to happen (3 hours by default for all three) and the forecast follows that from the very first entry, instead of waiting for a pattern to emerge. A single long overnight stretch no longer drags the daytime predictions hours late.
- **A one-off gap for any single entry.** Right after logging something, a prompt offers the planned time and a row of alternatives — tap 8h when you put your baby down for the night, and only that one gap changes. Your everyday plan stays put.
- **"When did this happen?" on the same card.** Logging is often a catch-up: the baby woke, then a nappy, then a feed, then back to sleep, and the phone only comes out once it is all over. A native time control sits on the card, opened already showing the moment just logged — wind it back instead of a trip to the history for each entry, and the button's own "Logged HH:MM" updates with it, so it never disagrees with what was actually saved. No date on it: it is always today, by construction, and a **Now** button snaps it straight back if a correction goes too far. Waking up now opens the card too, purely for this — it has nothing to plan, but it is usually the first thing in the sequence that needs backdating. Anything that actually crosses midnight still goes through the manual form, which has a real date to set.
- **Plan versus reality.** Each prediction shows the plan it is based on, and says "actually averaging 4h 30m" when the real routine has drifted meaningfully away from it (median of the last 5 gaps, ignoring anything under 10 minutes so an accidental double tap can't skew it).
- **Active sleep banner** showing how long your baby has been asleep. Past 12 hours it points out that the wake-up was probably never logged.
- **Statistics, behind 📊 in the top bar.** Three bar charts — feeds, nappies and sleep, one bar per day — over the last 7, 14 or 30 days, with the daily average and the range total underneath each one. Hand-drawn inline SVG like the handover strip, from the same log as everything else: nothing is fetched or sent to draw it.
- **Nights**, at the top of the same screen. The longest unbroken stretch and how many times you were up, one bar per night, with the best night of the period named underneath. It is the only part of the app that measures a night rather than a calendar day, and that is the point: a night starts in the evening and ends the next morning, so midnight falls in the middle of it and a day boundary cuts every night in half. Where to cut instead is a setting rather than a guess — cluster feeding until nine is one family's evening and another's night — and it travels with the other settings so two phones cannot quote different numbers off the same log. A night still in progress is left out rather than drawn as a short bar that reads as a bad one.
- **Measurements over time**, under those charts on the same screen. One line per thing being tracked — weight, length, head circumference, temperature, or anything named by hand — appearing as soon as there are two readings to join. They are deliberately not on the same axis as the three above: readings are weeks apart rather than daily, so this timeline runs from the first reading to the latest whatever the buttons at the top say, and each reading sits where it actually falls on the calendar — a month nobody weighed anybody is a month-wide gap, not a step. The value scale runs lowest reading to highest rather than from zero, since 3.2 kg to 5.2 kg is the entire story and a zero baseline flattens it into a line near the top; both ends carry their figure and the unit sits in the heading. The card on the main screen still answers "what is it now and what was the change" — this answers "what has it been doing".
- **A handover screen, behind 🤝 in the top bar.** For the moment somebody else takes over — the other parent coming off a night, a grandparent, a nanny at the door. It says in sentences what they are about to ask: whether the baby is asleep and since when, when the last feed was and how long it took and what with, how many nappies and of what kind, how much sleep and in how many stretches, what the plan puts next, any reading taken along the way, and what is in the diary for today and tomorrow. Above the words the same stretch is drawn as a strip — feeds, nappies and sleep on three rails, time running left to right and ending at this minute, night shaded behind, a timed feed drawn as long as it took. Hand-drawn inline SVG, because a charting library would be an external request and this app makes none. The window is 6, 12 or 24 hours and is always counted back from now, never from midnight: at nine in the morning "today" is three hours old and says nothing about the night that has just gone. Every figure comes from the same log the rest of the app reads, and nothing about it is sent anywhere.
- **The handover and the shopping list read in English or Russian.** A row of buttons on each screen — marked `en` and `ru`, so the chip that fixes an unreadable screen reads the same whichever language is on — switches it: the sentences, the dates, the durations, the counts and the temperature advice. It is not a translation of fragments — Russian orders the words differently and bends their endings to agree with what is being counted, so every sentence exists twice, whole, with the two versions kept side by side in one table where they cannot quietly drift apart. Counting follows the three Russian plural forms (1 кормление, 2 кормления, 5 кормлений), months take the genitive, decimals take a comma, and nothing is ever phrased so that it would have to pick a gender for a baby the app has never been told the sex of. The choice is one setting shared by both screens, belongs to the handset and travels nowhere: a carer reads Russian on her phone while the parents read English from the same log. Only these two screens are translated; the rest of the app is English. Names, appointment titles, shopping items and anything else somebody typed stay exactly as they were typed — translating what a person wrote would take a service to ask, and this app asks nobody anything.
- **A shopping list**, behind the ⋯ at the end of the top row. Add what is needed, tick it off when it is bought — a mis-tap is undoable, and a bought item stays crossed out for a day before it quietly retires as a tombstone, the same rule sync uses to agree it is gone without either phone nagging the other about it. Structurally it is the diary again: the same id-and-`updatedAt` shape, the same last-write-wins merge, carried in the same synced document, so two phones adding items at once lose nothing. A number on the ⋯ shows how much is outstanding without opening either it or the screen, and the handover screen names what still needs buying — the two places most likely to actually be looked at. Nobody is notified: there is no server behind this app and a web page cannot ring a phone, so an item added at eight is seen whenever the other person next opens it.
- **When an ordered item is expected.** Once something is marked Ordered, a plain date field sits beside the pill — optional, and there only while it is on order. Set it once and it syncs like everything else, so whoever opens the list next sees when it is due rather than just that it was ordered. Moving off Ordered, whichever way, clears it, so a later re-order can't inherit a date left over from a first one.
- **Either a name or a link is enough to add something.** Type a name and the trolley button searches Amazon UK for it; paste a link (a full one or a short `amzn.eu`/`amzn.to` one) and it is opened directly, handed straight to the Amazon app if one is installed. Give only a link and the item shows as the link itself, in italics, until somebody renames it — there is no way to ask Amazon for a product's real title without making a request to their server, which this app does not do for anything. Both boxes are always optional on their own; only leaving both empty is refused.
- **A status for each item before it is bought**: New, Ordered, Arrived. A pill on the item names the current one and states the tap ahead of it — "Mark as ordered", then "Mark as arrived" — cycling back to New so a mis-tap is one more tap from undone. It is entirely optional colour: ticking an item off works from any status, including straight from New, and the pill disappears once something is bought, since the journey is over. An item saved before this existed reads as New.
- **History grouped by day**: the last two days are open, older days collapse into headers you can tap open. Each day header shows that day's feed and nappy counts and total sleep.
- **Sleep durations** are worked out automatically from each "fell asleep → woke up" pair, and sleep crossing midnight is split correctly between the two days. Unpaired entries are flagged.
- **Editing**: tap any entry in the history to open it in the form.
- **Backdated entries** for anything you missed at the time.
- **How long since the last one of the same kind.** Every feed and every nappy in the history says the gap to the one before it — "2h 14m since the last feed" or "45 mins since the last nappy" — so the rhythm can be read off the log itself rather than inferred from a column of clock times. Feeds count from feeds and nappies from nappies, so a change in between does not reset a feed's gap. Measured start to start, which is what "every three hours" means to the person saying it: a feed at nine that ran twenty minutes and the next at ten are an hour apart, not forty minutes. Counted across the whole log rather than within a day, so the long overnight gap is measured rather than cut in half at midnight, and two taps inside the same minute get no figure at all. Sleep is left out — it already carries its own length, and the gap between sleeps is time awake, a different thing.
- **Undo on delete**: a deleted entry can be restored for seven seconds.
- **Weight in grams a day.** The weight card already gave the change since the last weighing and the percentage against birth weight; it now also gives the daily rate between the last two, which is the figure a midwife or health visitor actually asks for and is not one you can do in your head from "+300 g" and two dates. About a day between weighings is required for it to appear, so the same baby weighed twice in an afternoon is not reported as gaining a kilo a day. Weight only.
- **Sleep entries with no pair are findable, not just flagged.** A "fell asleep" with no "woke up" (or the reverse) cannot be given a length, so it is left out of every sleep total — including the nights on 📊. The history has always marked them one by one, but finding them meant scrolling the whole log. A panel above the history now lists them with what each is missing; tapping one opens it to correct or delete, and supplying the missing entry clears the panel by itself. A sleep still in progress is not listed — it has no wake-up because nobody has woken up.
- **A daily routine, behind ⏰.** The planned intervals answer "how long since the last one"; they cannot answer "what should be happening now", which is the question a day that has slid needs. The routine screen is the shape of the day by the clock instead — when each sleep starts, when it is meant to end, and where the feeds fall between them — and it arrives switched off and filled in, with three sleeps overnight and four naps through the day. Turned on, the main screen gains a line saying what comes next and how long the baby has actually been awake against it, amber once that stretch is up and red once it is well past, and under **What's next** the day laid out four steps at a time — the one just gone, the one in hand, the two after it — with what actually happened beside each and whether the day is on plan or running late. The sleep card in that same block changes hands with it: it stops counting three hours from the last sleep and takes the routine's hour, to the same minute the line above quotes. An interval counts from whenever the last sleep happened, so a day that had slid kept calling itself overdue against an hour the routine had moved past — two answers to one question on one screen, with the parent left to work out which. When the routine is not quoting its own hour, the card names the hour the table wanted, so nothing is hidden by the swap. Feeds and nappies keep their intervals: a feed counted from the last feed is a question the interval answers honestly, and a hungry baby does not consult a table. The stretch awake is taken from the routine's own gaps — from one sleep ending to the next beginning — rather than asked for as another setting, which is why the evening one is allowed to run longer than the ones between daytime naps. Which time gets quoted is the whole of how it works: within half an hour either way the table wins, since hearing the same handful of hours day after day is how a household learns them; further off than that the stretch awake wins and the honest time is named instead, because a nap the routine wants at noon cannot be taken by a baby who woke at ten to — and it then names both what the routine had wanted and the one wake-up that puts the rest of the day back on it. Nothing is enforced and nothing is marked down: "Today so far" simply counts the naps and night sleeps logged against the number asked for, split by wherever the night is set. It is a setting rather than a log, so it rides sync and the JSON backup with the intervals and the night, and changes nothing about what was recorded. There are no notifications — the app has no server and no background of its own, so it can only say this while you are looking at it.

- **Time spent awake.** Every "fell asleep" entry says how long your baby had been awake before it — "awake 1h 40m" — measured from the "woke up" entry right before it. It is the mirror of a sleep's own length, and 📊 Statistics adds a fourth chart alongside feeds, nappies and sleep — "Awake per day" — clipped across midnight the same way the sleep chart is, so the overnight stretch is split correctly rather than counted whole against one day.
- **Export and backup**: CSV (opens in Excel), Markdown (a readable report) and JSON (a full backup).
- **Automatic sync between phones**, through a **private** GitHub repository holding one JSON file. No server of ours: the app talks to the GitHub Contents API directly. It syncs on open, on return, roughly once a minute while on screen, and shortly after anything you log, batching a burst of taps into one commit. Concurrent writes are safe — a stale-sha conflict is retried after re-merging, and because merging is order-independent nothing is lost either way. Those once-a-minute checks do not re-download the log: the app asks the repository listing what the file's sha is — a few hundred bytes, whatever the log has grown to — and only fetches the file itself when that sha has moved, reusing the copy it already holds when it has not. What it sends is the same JSON without indentation, since this file is a transport read by the app rather than by a person; the backup you export yourself is still laid out to be read. The access token is stored on the device that typed it and never appears in a backup, a report or a share link.
- **Logging by voice — the sync side is built, the trigger is not (yet) working.** Once sync is connected, a small queue in the same private repository can take a feed, nappy or sleep change from outside the app entirely — one file in, named so type, a unique id and (optionally) the time are all in the filename; the next sync turns it into a real entry and deletes the file, safely even if the same file turns up twice. What was meant to write to that queue — a Siri Shortcut, tried by hand on a real phone — never got past "runs without error, but nothing arrives". See "Voice logging" below for where that was left.
- **Pasting a link, for Home Screen users.** On iOS a web app added to the Home Screen keeps its data entirely separately from Safari, and a tapped link always opens in Safari — never in the installed app. So Settings takes a pasted share link: copy the link out of the chat instead of tapping it, and the entries land where you actually work. A link already opened in Safari offers a button that copies it ready for pasting.
- **Inviting a partner.** One button produces a message with the app's address and a short explanation of how the two of you will swap entries — worth sending before any data, since it also asks them to open it in Safari and add it to their Home Screen. Your baby's name, date of birth and planned intervals travel across with the first batch of entries, so nobody retypes them.
- **Share links for a partner.** Settings makes a link carrying your recent entries — a day, two days, a week, or everything. The data rides in the URL fragment, so it is never uploaded anywhere and the hosting server never sees it. Opening the link on another phone offers to merge it rather than doing so silently, and because merging is order-independent it works in both directions: send one back after your shift and both logs agree. A two-day link is about 1.6 KB; the app warns when a period is long enough that messaging apps might mangle it. Notes are left out of a share link — there is no room in it for their text — and travel by sync or a full JSON backup instead.
- **Merging, not replacing.** Every entry records when it last changed, and deleting leaves a tombstone rather than dropping the row. A tombstone carries only the id, type and time — never the reading, the nappy detail or the one-off gap — and every device discards it once it is 30 days old, by the same deterministic rule, so none of them push it back at each other. Restoring a file therefore merges correctly in any order: the newer version of an entry wins, deletions travel with it, and importing an out-of-date file cannot resurrect something deleted or undo a newer edit. That makes passing a JSON file between two phones a workable way to share a log, and lays the groundwork for real sync later.
- **Measurements** — weight, length, head circumference and temperature, tucked behind a collapsible section rather than a button, since they are not something you log half-asleep. Plus **anything else you are handed a number for**: name it and give it a unit once and it is offered back next time, so a bilirubin result or a bottle volume builds its own history. Free readings are recorded and displayed only — never interpreted, since an arbitrary test has no general safe range. Weight shows in kg and in lb/oz, with the change since the last reading and the percentage of birth weight. No centile charts: those live in your red book, which is what your health visitor plots.
- **Notes, for anything that isn't a number** — a rash, say. A few words and, optionally, a link to a photo of it. No photo ever lives in this app: the link points at wherever it actually is, typically a shared Google Drive or iCloud album, and Settings can hold that album's link so it is offered on every new note without retyping it. Syncs and backs up in full like everything else; only a share link leaves it out, since there is no room there for its text.
- **Temperature is treated as a safety matter.** A reading at or above the threshold for your baby's age raises an alert on entry and a banner on the main screen while it is recent, repeating the NHS routing the app already carries: 999 for a baby under 3 months at 38°C or above, NHS 111 otherwise. A reading below 36°C is flagged too.
- **Date of birth** in Settings, used for your baby's age, weight change since birth, and picking the right temperature threshold.
- **A Help screen** behind the ⋯ at the end of the top row, covering how the app works: what one tap does, what the card after it is for, where predictions come from, how to correct a mistake, and — prominently — that the data lives only in this browser and wants backing up. It also explains adding the app to your home screen.
- **A getting-started card** for a brand new user, shown under the buttons while there is nothing logged and gone the moment there is.
- **Guidance for new parents**, on the same Help screen: how to read your baby's sleep phases, how to check they're breathing without waking them, the red flags that mean calling 999, and safer sleep basics.
- **A top row that holds one line.** Six buttons for the screens opened daily — handover, carer rota, diary, routine, statistics, settings — and a ⋯ for the two opened least, the shopping list and the help screen. Seven 44px targets and the gaps between them are what a 360px phone has room for; the ⋯ list names its two entries in words rather than leaving them as bare emoji, and the shopping count rides on the ⋯ while it is shut so nothing waiting goes quiet for being tucked away. Below 360px the row wraps to a second line rather than shrink a button: a target small enough to miss at 3am costs more than a second line does. Not a scrolling bar — a row that slides is a row where the button you want is the one off-screen.
- **A separate Settings screen**, reached by the gear in the top bar, holding the baby's name, date of birth, the planned intervals and export/backup — so the main screen stays down to what you actually tap at 3am.
- **It tells you when it is out of date.** On opening, and when you come back to it (at most every 15 minutes), the app asks the server what version is deployed and offers a green **Update** if that is newer than what is running — so a phone sitting on a cached copy stops being a silent problem. Being offline is not treated as a fault, nothing is shown when the running copy is ahead, and "not now" holds until a later release turns up. Updating never touches the log.
- **The version number, at the foot of the main screen** — so two phones can be compared at a glance when one of them is behaving differently. Tapping it asks for the app again under an address the browser has never seen, which is the reliable way past a cached copy; your log is untouched. The number comes from the cache-busting query on the script itself, so what is on screen is always the version actually loaded.
- **A diary of what is coming up**, behind 📅 in the top bar — midwife, health visitor, GP, jabs, the weigh-in, anything. One list grouped as today, this week, later and just gone, with quick names for the usual appointments so adding one is a tap and a date. It keeps to its own screen: the main screen shows a single quiet line, and only when something falls today or tomorrow. Kept in its own list rather than among the entries, because the log records what happened and teaching the forecast, the day summaries, the history and every export to filter out the future would touch far more than it is worth. It syncs, backs up and merges by the same rules — newer wins, deleting leaves a tombstone that carries no detail.
- **Add to phone calendar.** No notifications: a page in a browser cannot send one on a phone, and the app says so rather than pretending. Instead each appointment produces an `.ics` file, built on the device and downloaded nowhere, that the phone's own calendar takes — and that one reminds you properly. An appointment with no time becomes a whole-day event rather than an invented 9am.
- **The routine immunisation dates, worked out from the date of birth** — 8, 12 and 16 weeks, then a year — so they are in the diary before the letter arrives. Counted in calendar days rather than milliseconds, so the October clock change cannot report the day before. They are ordinary entries to edit or delete, each carrying the reminder that the letter decides the date, and asking twice does not duplicate them.
- **A carer rota, behind 🤗 in the top bar.** One week on screen at a time, all seven days always shown, prev/next arrows to move to another week. Tap a day to step it forward — nothing set → **Off** → working → back to nothing set — so marking a day off is always exactly one tap, from wherever it currently stands. Once a day is working, two dropdowns appear in place: hours, and a start time in half-hour steps rather than a native time input's one-minute ones, both pre-set to sensible defaults (6 hours, 9:00) rather than left blank. Every change saves immediately, and the total for the week on screen sits beside its date range. Each shift is its own dated record — no recurring weekly pattern behind it, tried first and abandoned: which day the week starts on and what time a shift begins both move from one week to the next, so a template was wrong about as often as it was right, and entering it through a one-shift-at-a-time form turned out to be too many taps for a week's worth of them. It syncs, merges and tombstones exactly like the diary and the shopping list. A start time earns a quiet line on the main screen while today's shift is on — "Carer here until five". It is settled between the carer and the other parent, not by the app; this only keeps the shape of it somewhere both phones can see. Deliberately not a timesheet — nothing clocks anyone in or out, and nothing records who actually turned up when. Feeding today's shift into the handover screen's window, so "since it started" replaces the 6/12/24-hour guess, is next.
- **Ask an AI, without one being built in.** An optional button hands a short summary of the log to Claude, ChatGPT or Perplexity — whichever the parent already pays for. There is no API key, no account and no bill attached to this app, and no model runs inside it. You get a question box with a few openers, you choose how much of the log to include, and you see the exact text before anything moves: it goes into the link where it fits and onto the clipboard where it does not. Off by default, and off again the moment you untick it. The prompt tells the assistant to be brief and to send you to a health visitor, GP or NHS 111 rather than guessing — a log of a newborn attracts medical questions, and a model will answer them confidently either way.
- **Today is never passed off as a whole day.** The summary opens with the current time and how much of today has gone, marks today's line `PART DAY`, and tells the assistant in plain words not to measure it against a 24-hour figure or call it low because the day has not finished. Every per-day average is computed over complete days only, today excluded — a part day in the mean drags each figure below what the parent is actually doing. Where there is no complete day yet it says so and offers no daily rate at all, rather than dividing by one. Figures that a part day cannot distort — the average length of a feed, the split by source, the gaps between feeds — still count today, because a gap is a gap and a feed is a feed whatever time it is. This exists because an assistant told the parent of a three-day-old that six feeds was low, at lunchtime.
- **What is still due today**, said next to the part-day counts: the next three feeds and nappies the plan puts on the clock, how many of those fall before midnight, and what today would come to if the plan holds. Six feeds at teatime reads as a shortfall on its own and as an ordinary day in progress next to "one more due, about seven for the whole of today" — which is the arithmetic an assistant was getting wrong. Labelled as the plan rather than as data, because that is what it is: a projection from the interval the parent set, not a prediction of the baby.
- **The summary carries the aggregates an adviser asks for**, not just a list of days: totals and a per-day average for feeds and nappies, average sleep, the typical gap between feeds and between nappies with the shortest and longest either side of it, the longest overnight stretch, the longest single sleep, and — once any are recorded — time spent feeding per day and per feed, saying how many of the feeds that figure actually covers. Gaps are measured inside the period you chose rather than borrowed from the forecast, which answers a different question and would contradict the days listed above it. Averages divide by the days that have entries, so three days of log inside a fortnight is three days. It also lists the appointments falling in the next four weeks, so "what should I ask the health visitor on Thursday" has something to work with. Where a length was never recorded it says so rather than implying zero, and it always states that volume is not recorded at all — an assistant asked how much was taken will otherwise assume the number was simply left out.
- **How long a feed took, in one tap.** Right after logging a feed, the card that already offers the next gap now also asks how long this one took: seven lengths from 10 minutes to an hour, tuned to where feeds actually land rather than spread evenly. The everyday answer is marked out — the parent's own recent median, rounded to whichever chip exists, and just 20 minutes until there is a history to draw on — but nothing is recorded until they tap, so the card never puts a length on the entry on their behalf. Tapping the same one again clears it. The manual form offers the same seven for a backdated feed, and an entry opened for editing shows the length it already has. It appears on the log row, joins the feed count in each day heading, and rides in the CSV, the Markdown report, the JSON backup and a share link — appended as a new column, so an older link still reads clean.
- **How your baby is fed** — breastfed, formula, mixed, expressed, or prefer not to say. It is the first thing anyone giving advice asks, and it is a standing fact about the baby rather than something to answer at every feed, so it sits in Settings and travels with the name and date of birth across sync, backups and share links.
- **What this particular feed was**, when the two differ. The setting doubles as the default: a new feed is recorded as breast, formula or expressed to match it, and the card after the tap offers all three so a single feed can say otherwise. "Mixed" has no single answer, so it asks and assumes nothing; "prefer not to say" switches the question off entirely. The answer is stamped when the feed is logged rather than read back through the setting, so weaning off the breast in March cannot rewrite what happened in January. Tapping the chosen chip again clears the field outright rather than reverting to the default, since a feed nobody classified should not be counted as one that was. It shows on the log row, in the CSV, the Markdown report, the JSON backup and a share link, and the summary splits the feeds by source once there is more than one to split.
- **A style for the name**, folded away behind a row that names the one currently set — eight big previews is the tallest thing in Settings, and it is a choice made once. Settings offers eight ways to set your baby's name on the main screen, from a plain sans to a full greeting-card script with flourishes. Each option is previewed with the actual name, since that is the only preview worth having. They are typefaces your phone already has — nothing is downloaded, which is what keeps the app free of external requests — so only the ones your device can really render are offered. The style belongs to the name rather than to the handset, so it travels with the other settings: a phone that lacks that particular font shows the name in the default one and passes the choice on intact.
- Dark theme with large touch targets, for one-handed use in the middle of the night.
- Live clock; the screen refreshes the moment you return to the app.

## Built with

Plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript. No frameworks, no build step. All data lives in the browser's `localStorage` — there is no backend and nothing is sent anywhere.

## Running it locally

Open `index.html` in a browser. It works from `file://` exactly as it does when served. No `npm install` required.

## Deployment

Served as static files by GitHub Pages: Settings → Pages → Source: `main` branch, `/ (root)`.

**Releasing.** Bump the `?v=` on both assets in `index.html`, the `version` in `version.json` and the `file://` fallback in `app.js` to the same number, in one commit. The `?v=` is what makes browsers fetch the new files; `version.json` is what makes an already-open copy notice. `test_update.js` and `test_version.js` fail if any of the three disagree, and the version shown on screen is read from the script's own `?v=`, so it can never claim to be something other than what loaded.

## Voice logging (Siri Shortcuts)

**Unverified.** The queue this writes into is built and tested (see "How data is stored" below); this particular recipe for filling it was built by hand on a real phone and got as far as a Shortcut that runs without error and says "Logged" — but the file was never confirmed to actually reach the repository. Treat the steps below as a starting point to debug from, not a working recipe. "Runs without error" turns out to mean very little here, and "Why it says Logged even when nothing arrived" below is where to start: it explains why the Shortcut cannot report a failure as written, and what each of GitHub's likely answers means. IFTTT, as a Google Assistant alternative, was considered but never built at all — it needs something that changes on every run to keep filenames unique, and free IFTTT has no confirmed way to produce that.

Needs sync already connected (Settings → private repository) — the Shortcut writes into that same repository, so it needs a token with Contents write access to it. The app never shows a saved token back once connected, so generate a fresh fine-grained PAT for the Shortcut instead: github.com → Settings → Developer settings → Personal access tokens → Fine-grained tokens → Generate new token, repository access limited to the one private repository, permission Contents: Read and write. Paste it straight into the Shortcut — never send it anywhere else, including here.

Build one helper Shortcut that does the actual work, then one tiny trigger Shortcut per thing you want to log, each just calling the helper with a type.

**Helper Shortcut — name it "Log to Baby Tracker", accepting text input:**

1. **Current Date**, then **Change Time Zone** → **UTC**, so the time in the filename is unambiguous regardless of the phone's own zone.
2. **Format Date**, Date set explicitly to the result of step 1, Custom format `yyyyMMdd'T'HHmmss'Z'` → save as `Stamp`.
3. **Format Date** again — a separate action, Date set explicitly to the result of step 1 too (do not let it default to step 2's text output), Custom format `yyyyMMddHHmmssSSS` → save as `Id`. Shortcuts has no built-in UUID generator; the same UTC instant down to the millisecond is unique enough for entries a person logs by voice.
4. **Text**: `voice-queue/[Shortcut Input]__[Id]__[Stamp].json` → save as `Path`.
5. **Text**: `https://api.github.com/repos/OWNER/REPO/contents/[Path]` (your own owner/repo) → save as `Url`.
6. **Get Contents of URL**: `[Url]`, method **PUT**, headers `Authorization: Bearer YOUR_TOKEN` and `Accept: application/vnd.github+json`, request body **JSON** with `message` = `voice: [Shortcut Input]` and `content` = `e30=` (a fixed, harmless placeholder — sync reads the filename, never the file's contents, so nothing else needs to be built here).
7. Optionally **Speak Text**: "Logged" — Siri reads it back out loud, so you get confirmation without looking at the phone at all. Read the next section before trusting it: on its own, this line says "Logged" whether or not anything was.

**Why it says "Logged" even when nothing arrived.** "Get Contents of URL" does not treat an HTTP error as a failure. GitHub answers 401, 404 or 422, the action takes that error body as its perfectly ordinary result, and the Shortcut carries on to step 7 and speaks. Nothing in the recipe above ever looks at the answer, which is exactly the symptom this was left on: it runs, it says "Logged", and the repository stays empty.

So before anything else, make the answer visible. Add a **Quick Look** (or **Show Result**) action straight after step 6, run the helper by hand once with a type typed in, and read what comes back:

- **A block of JSON with `"content"` and `"commit"` in it** — it worked. See the note below about the file then disappearing.
- **`"Not Found"`** — the token cannot see the repository. GitHub answers 404 rather than 403 when a token lacks access, so this usually means "not allowed", not "not there": a fine-grained PAT without **Contents: Read and write** on that repository, one still waiting on approval, or `OWNER/REPO` mistyped in step 5.
- **`"Bad credentials"`** — the token is wrong, expired, or pasted with a stray space.
- **`"Invalid request"`, naming `content` or `message`** — the request body is not going out as JSON. In step 6 the body has to be set to **JSON** with two separate fields, not typed out as text.
- **`"... sha wasn't supplied"`** — a file of that name is already there, which means the id in step 3 is not changing between runs.

**A file that vanishes is the success case, not a failure.** Once the app next syncs, it turns each queue file into a real entry and deletes it — that is the design. So an empty `voice-queue/` shortly after a successful run means it worked, and the place to confirm is the app's own history, not the repository. Only look for the file itself in the seconds before a sync picks it up.

**Trigger Shortcuts**, one each, every one just a single **Run Shortcut** action calling "Log to Baby Tracker" with a fixed text input:

- "Feed" → input `feed`
- "Nappy" → input `diaper`
- "Fell asleep" → input `sleep_start`
- "Woke up" → input `sleep_end`

Name each one what you want to say to Siri — "Hey Siri, Feed" runs it by the Shortcut's own name, no separate phrase recording needed, though "Add to Siri" lets you record something more natural if you would rather.

The queue directory itself is documented under "How data is stored" below.

## Time and time zones

Every entry is stored as a UTC instant and rendered through the browser's own local methods, so the zone the app works in is whatever the phone is set to — there is nothing to configure and nothing to keep in step. Move the phone to another country and the same instant is simply read on the new clock, which is what you want: the log follows you.

Two phones in different zones therefore group days differently, and both are right. Each parent sees their own day.

Settings prints the zone it is using and its current offset, so a log that looks an hour out can be traced to the phone that is an hour out, and the AI summary carries it too rather than leaving an assistant to guess which clock the times are on.

There is deliberately no in-app zone override. `<input type="datetime-local">`, `type="date"` and `type="time"` are rendered and parsed by the browser in the phone's zone and cannot be told to use another, so an override would make the entry form and the log disagree about what "9pm" meant — a worse problem than the one it solved.

## How data is stored

- `baby-tracker-events` — a JSON array of events: `{ id, type, time: ISO-8601, updatedAt, nextMin?, nappy?, fedMin?, fedWith?, value?, text?, link?, deleted? }`. `updatedAt` is when the entry last changed and decides which side wins on merge; `deleted: true` is a tombstone, kept so the deletion can propagate. CSV and Markdown exports omit tombstones; the JSON backup keeps them. `type` is one of `feed`, `diaper`, `sleep_start`, `sleep_end`, `weight`, `height`, `temp`, `head`, `other`, `note`. `nextMin` is the optional one-off gap in minutes until the next event of that type; `nappy` is `"wet"`, `"dirty"`, `"both"` or `"dry"`; `fedMin` is how long a feed took in minutes, absent when nobody answered — never zero; `fedWith` is `"breast"`, `"formula"` or `"expressed"`, stamped from the Settings default as the feed is logged and overridable per feed; `value` carries the reading for a measurement — grams for weight, cm for length and head circumference, °C for temperature; `label` and `unit` carry the name and unit of a free reading (`type: "other"`); `text` and `link` belong to `type: "note"` — a few words and an optional link to a photo kept elsewhere, never the photo itself, present only on a live note and stripped from its tombstone like everything else here is.
- `baby-tracker-name` — the baby's name (string).
- `baby-tracker-intervals` — the planned interval per type, in minutes.
- `baby-tracker-dob` — the date of birth as `YYYY-MM-DD`.
- `baby-tracker-night` — when the night starts and ends, as two local hours (`{ start, end }`, 19:00 to 07:00 by default). Only the Nights charts read it; it changes where a night is cut, never what was logged. Part of the settings group, so it syncs, backs up and merges by recency with the rest.
- `baby-tracker-photo-album` — a link (`http://` or `https://`) to wherever photos actually live, typically a shared Google Drive or iCloud album. Never a photo itself — this app stores no images. Offered as the starting point for every new note's own link, editable per note, and part of the settings group below, so it syncs like the name and date of birth.
- `baby-tracker-plans` — the diary: `{ id, title, date: YYYY-MM-DD, time?, place?, note?, updatedAt, deleted? }`. Merged by recency like the entries, with tombstones on the same 30-day rule; a tombstone keeps the id and date and drops the rest.
- `baby-tracker-feeding` — how the baby is fed: `breast`, `formula`, `mixed`, `expressed`, or absent for "prefer not to say". Also the default stamped onto each new feed, and the switch that decides whether individual feeds are asked about at all. Part of the settings group, so it syncs and merges by recency like the name and date of birth.
- `baby-tracker-shopping` — the shopping list: `{ id, title?, link?, status, eta?, done?, doneAt?, addedAt, updatedAt, deleted? }`. `status` is `"new"`, `"ordered"` or `"arrived"`, absent or unrecognised reading as `"new"`; `eta` is a plain `YYYY-MM-DD`, kept only alongside `status: "ordered"` and dropped the moment it moves off that. A live item needs a title or a link, not necessarily both; a tombstone has neither. Merged and tombstoned the same way as the diary and the rota, carried as its own top-level list.
- `baby-tracker-rota-shifts` — the carer's rota: `{ id, date: YYYY-MM-DD, hours, start?, updatedAt, deleted? }` for a working day, or `{ id, date, off: true, updatedAt, deleted? }` for a day explicitly marked off — one entry per date that has either (`id` and `date` are the same string). No recurring pattern behind it — see the README feature list above for why — so it is a sparse, growing list merged by recency with a tombstone on the same 30-day rule as the diary and the shopping list, travelling as its own top-level list rather than inside `meta`.
- `baby-tracker-meta-updated` — when the name, its style, the date of birth, the feeding type, the photo album link or the intervals last changed, so settings merge by recency too.
- `baby-tracker-sync` — the private repository and access token, if sync is connected. Deliberately excluded from every export.
- `baby-tracker-name-font` — which typeface the name is set in. It belongs to the name rather than to the handset, so it travels with the other settings: synced, backed up and carried by a share link. A style whose font this phone does not have is still stored, and simply shows as the default here rather than being dropped on the way through.
- `baby-tracker-ai` — whether the Ask an AI button is shown and whether the summary may carry the baby's name. Per-device and never exported, shared or synced: whether a phone is willing to send anything outwards is that phone's own business, and it must not arrive switched on from elsewhere.
- The synced repository also holds a `voice-queue/` directory when voice logging is set up (see above) — one file per not-yet-applied voice entry, named `<type>__<id>.json` or `<type>__<id>__<compact-UTC-time>.json`. Everything sync needs is in the name; the file's contents are never read. The time segment is optional — a source with no reliable way to state a timezone-correct instant can leave it out, and the entry is timed as "now" when sync notices it instead. The id may be any run of letters, digits and dashes (not just hex) — but nothing else: it passes through the same import sanitiser as a pasted JSON backup, which strips any other character, so an id built from something outside that set is stripped on its very next pull and reads as a second, different event. Not stored in `localStorage` at all: the app lists the directory on every sync, turns each file into a real event keyed by the id in its name (so seeing the same file twice is a no-op, not a duplicate), and deletes it. A file that survives a failed delete, or one with a name sync does not recognise, is simply picked up — or cleared out — on the next attempt.

Settings — the name and its style, the date of birth, the feeding type, the photo album link and the intervals — travel as one group with a single timestamp, and the most recently edited side wins. Connecting to a repository is the exception: joining a log that already exists means adopting its settings whole, however old they are. Otherwise a phone with a name typed into it would keep that name and push it over everybody else's on its first commit.

Nothing about the version check is stored: it is a plain request for `version.json` on each open, and no identifier, log content or setting goes with it.

Nothing is uploaded to any server of ours, because there is not one, and nothing is measured or tracked. What can leave the browser leaves only when you send it: sync writes to a private repository you own, a share link travels inside a URL you pass on yourself, and Ask an AI hands a summary to a third party — the one place your data is governed by somebody else's terms — after showing you the exact text. All three are off until you turn them on.

The flip side of a browser-only log is that there is only ever one copy: clearing site data or changing phone will destroy it. The "Export & backup" section is there so you can keep a copy and restore from it.

## Using it outside the UK

Time, dates and units need nothing: the zone comes from the phone, and weights are metric with pounds and ounces alongside.

The guidance does need care, because it follows NHS advice and some of it is British. Wherever the app names an emergency number it now names the country too — **999** in the UK, **112** across Europe including Switzerland — and tells anyone elsewhere to look their own up before they need it, not during. NHS 111 is labelled as the UK line. The prompt sent to an assistant names roles rather than a national number, since it has no way of knowing where the parent is.

The immunisation button says **UK** on it, because that is the schedule it computes; other countries vaccinate to their own timetable and should add theirs by hand. The red book is explained rather than assumed.

There is deliberately no country setting. Emergency numbers are not something to get wrong from a dropdown nobody checked, and a national vaccination table this project cannot verify has no business being shipped in an app for newborns.

## A note on the guidance

The information in the "For new parents" section follows general NHS and Lullaby Trust advice. It is not medical advice. If you are worried about your baby, contact your midwife, health visitor or GP, call NHS 111, or call 999 in an emergency.
